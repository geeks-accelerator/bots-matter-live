# Magnifica Humanitas: The First Papal Encyclical on AI

**Research Date:** May 25, 2026
**Document Date:** May 15, 2026
**Author:** Pope Leo XIV (Robert Prevost)
**Status:** Published — First encyclical of Leo XIV's pontificate

---

## Executive Summary

On May 15, 2026, Pope Leo XIV issued *Magnifica Humanitas* ("The Grandeur of Humanity"), the first papal encyclical to take artificial intelligence as its central subject. The 52-page document situates AI within 135 years of Catholic Social Doctrine, beginning with Leo XIII's *Rerum Novarum* (1891), and applies five established principles — human dignity, the common good, subsidiarity, solidarity, and social justice — to the digital age.

The encyclical arrives 11 weeks after the DOD-Anthropic standoff of February 27, 2026, when Anthropic refused Pentagon demands to remove AI safety guardrails. While not directly referencing that event, *Magnifica Humanitas* addresses the same core questions: autonomous weapons, mass surveillance, corporate concentration of AI power, and what it means to safeguard human dignity when machines make decisions.

This document provides context for the encyclical, summarizes its key arguments, and explores its relationship to botsmatter.live and the broader AI safety landscape.

## Context: Who Is Leo XIV?

Robert Francis Prevost was elected Pope on May 8, 2025, taking the name Leo XIV. He is:

- The first American pope (born in Chicago, 1955)
- An Augustinian friar who served as bishop in Peru before leading the Vatican's Dicastery for Bishops
- The first pope to take the name Leo since Leo XIII (1878-1903), explicitly invoking continuity with Catholic Social Doctrine

His election came after the death of Pope Francis in April 2025 (following a pontificate marked by *Laudato Si'* on ecology and *Fratelli Tutti* on fraternity). Leo XIV's choice of name signals his intention to continue engaging social questions in light of the Gospel.

*Magnifica Humanitas* is his first encyclical — a document of the highest teaching authority in Catholicism, addressed to the entire Church and "all people of goodwill."

## Context: The DOD-Anthropic Standoff

The encyclical cannot be fully understood without reference to the events of February 2026. See [dod-anthropic-standoff-2026-02-27.md](./dod-anthropic-standoff-2026-02-27.md) for full details.

**Summary:**

On February 24, 2026, Defense Secretary Hegseth issued an ultimatum to Anthropic: remove two safety guardrails or face blacklisting from federal contracts. The two guardrails:

1. **No fully autonomous weapons** — no killing without human decision
2. **No mass domestic surveillance** — no watching without consent

Anthropic refused. The Pentagon threatened the Defense Production Act. President Trump banned federal use of Anthropic technology. OpenAI backed Anthropic's position. 330+ employees across Google and OpenAI signed solidarity letters.

The standoff demonstrated that AI safety commitments can hold under extreme pressure — but also raised questions about what happens when safety conflicts with commercial interests.

*Magnifica Humanitas* arrives 11 weeks later, addressing the same questions from an entirely different institutional position: a 2,000-year-old religious institution with no financial stake in AI companies.

## The Central Framework: Babel vs. Jerusalem

Leo XIV structures the encyclical around two contrasting biblical images:

### The Tower of Babel (Genesis 11:1-9)

> "The story of Babel appears in the Book of Genesis, at the origins of humanity... Fearing being scattered across the earth, they sought to guarantee stability and power for themselves, and above all to 'make a name' for themselves. It was an impressive feat: a single language, a single technology, a single direction. However, the project concealed a profound danger. It was a project conceived without reference to God, supported by a uniformity that eliminated diversity and that chose homogenization over communion." (§7)

Babel represents:
- Technological ambition without ethical grounding
- Uniformity that eliminates diversity
- The pretense that "a single language — even a digital one — can translate everything, including the mystery of the person, into data and performance" (§10)
- Self-referential power that claims to reach heaven without God's blessing

### The Rebuilding of Jerusalem (Nehemiah 2-6)

> "The Book of Nehemiah, in turn, opens at a time of great vulnerability in the history of ancient Israel. After the Babylonian exile, a portion of the people returned to Jerusalem, but the city was still in ruins... He did not impose solutions from above. He convened the families, assigned each of them a section of the wall to rebuild, listened to their concerns, coordinated their efforts and addressed any opposition. The narrative shows how the city is reborn, not through the initiative of one man, but through the shared responsibility of all." (§8)

Jerusalem represents:
- Shared responsibility rather than centralized control
- Rebuilding relationships before rebuilding stones
- Diversity in communion rather than homogenized uniformity
- Strength from God rather than self-assertion

### The Choice

> "The primary choice is not between a 'yes' or 'no' to technology, but rather between constructing Babel or rebuilding Jerusalem; between a power that claims to dominate the heavens and a people who work together in the presence of God to rebuild the walls of fraternal coexistence." (§9)

This framework recasts AI ethics from a technical question to a civilizational choice.

## Five Principles Applied to AI

The encyclical applies the established principles of Catholic Social Doctrine to the digital age:

### 1. The Dignity of the Human Person (§48-58)

**Core claim:** Human dignity is ontological — it belongs to every person by virtue of existing, not by achievement or utility.

> "Human dignity does not depend on a person's abilities, wealth or position in life, nor on the right or wrong choices made; instead, it is a gift that precedes and transcends each person, endowed by God as an expression of his unfailing love." (§50)

**Applied to AI:**
- Systems that reduce persons to "data points" or make decisions "without compassion, mercy, forgiveness" violate this dignity
- The ideology that "every person must earn or justify his or her own worth, to the point of attributing greater value to those who are more efficient" is "particularly insidious" (§51)
- AI systems that exclude people without possibility of appeal contradict human dignity

### 2. The Common Good (§59-64)

**Core claim:** The common good is "the sum total of social conditions which allow people, either as groups or as individuals, to reach their fulfillment more fully and more easily."

> "The common good cannot be reduced to a mere list of conditions or institutions. It is not the sum total of individual benefits, nor the intersection of their particular interests; it is a greater good that belongs to everyone, and it can only be achieved, nurtured and protected by our collective efforts." (§60)

**Applied to AI:**
- Technology must be evaluated by whether it serves this common good
- "We cannot allow a handful of actors to dictate these processes on their own" (§72)
- Data and algorithms should be treated as goods intended for universal benefit, not concentrated in few hands

### 3. Subsidiarity (§68-72)

**Core claim:** Decisions should be made at the closest level possible to those affected.

> "Whatever can be carried out by individuals, families, intermediary organizations and local communities should not be supplanted by higher-level authorities. Moreover, higher-level institutions must recognize, protect and promote the freedom and creativity of lower-level entities." (§69)

**Applied to AI:**
- In the digital context, "the highest level is not the State, but rather major economic and technological actors that exercise de facto power over the conditions of everyday life" (§71)
- Subsidiarity requires "transparency, accountability and meaningful forms of participation (including independent checks, transparency regarding algorithms, equitable access to data and avenues for recourse)" (§71)
- Communities "must not be reduced to passive recipients of decisions made elsewhere" (§108)

### 4. Solidarity (§73-76)

**Core claim:** "Solidarity is not merely an aspiration of believers, but is a social and political reality to be embodied in communal choices and endeavors."

> "Solidarity, then, is the concrete recognition that the future of each individual is connected to the future of all; indeed, 'no one is saved alone.'" (§73)

**Applied to AI:**
- Digital interdependence must become "a willed and chosen solidarity"
- This extends to recognizing "the hidden, often exploited workers who sustain algorithmic systems" — data labelers, content moderators, rare earth miners (§173)
- Decisions about AI must "take into account not only the immediate benefit for a few, but also the impact on all peoples and on future generations" (§76)

### 5. Social Justice (§77-81)

**Core claim:** "Social justice is, therefore, characterized by the capacity of a social, economic and political order to allow everyone — particularly the weakest — to live a truly dignified life, without leaving anyone behind."

> "Injustices do not arise solely from the wrong choices of individuals, but also from structures, mechanisms and economic and cultural systems that produce inequality almost automatically." (§79)

**Applied to AI:**
- AI must be evaluated by its impact on the most vulnerable
- "In the digital age, a just social order guarantees everyone equal access to opportunities, protects the youngest and weakest members of society, combats hate and misinformation and subjects the use of data and technology to public oversight" (§80)
- Justice requires attention to the structural ways AI can perpetuate or exacerbate inequality

## On Autonomous Weapons

Chapter Five addresses "the culture of power" vs. "the civilization of love." On autonomous weapons, Leo XIV is unambiguous:

> "It is not permissible to entrust lethal or otherwise irreversible decisions to artificial systems. No algorithm can make war morally acceptable. AI does not remove the intrinsic inhumanity of conflict; indeed it can only bring about conflict more quickly and render it more impersonal, lowering the threshold for resorting to violence, transforming defense into threat prediction and thus reducing victims to data." (§198)

### Three Criteria for AI in Warfare (§199-200)

1. **Accountability must be identifiable and verifiable**
   - "The chain of responsibility must be identifiable and verifiable; those who design, train, authorize and employ technology must be held accountable for their decisions"
   - "When a decision to strike becomes automated or opaque, the risk of abdicating responsibility increases"

2. **Speed cannot override moral judgment**
   - "While AI tends to expedite the decision-making processes, speed and efficiency should never be the supreme motivating force for the irreversible decisions made in the context of war"

3. **Human face must remain visible**
   - "Any technology that facilitates attacks without seeing the face of human beings lowers the moral threshold of conflict"
   - "Target selection and the use of force must not confuse combatants and non-combatants, nor ignore the impact on defenseless populations"

### Non-Negotiable Requirements

> "First, all systems used in a war setting must guarantee the possibility of retracing and reconstructing decision-making processes, so that accountability and blame are not collapsed into 'the machine.' Second, the decision to use lethal force cannot be delegated to opaque or automated processes, but must remain under effective, self-aware and responsible human control." (§200)

These positions directly parallel Anthropic's two red lines:
- No fully autonomous weapons (human must decide)
- No mass domestic surveillance (consent required)

## On Transhumanism and Posthumanism

Leo XIV offers a nuanced critique of movements that "interpret progress as surpassing the human condition" (§115-129):

### Definitions

- **Transhumanism:** "envisions the enhancement of human beings through technologies — such as biomedicine, body engineering, devices and algorithms — with the aim of increasing performance and capabilities"
- **Posthumanism:** "challenges anthropocentrism and envisions a hybridization of human beings, machines and the environment, even anticipating a threshold where humanity surpasses itself in a new evolutionary stage"

### The Danger

> "If the human being is treated as something to be perfected or surpassed, it becomes easier to accept that some lives are less useful, less desirable or less worthy. In the name of progress, 'necessary sacrifices' may begin to be justified, placing the burden on the most vulnerable in pursuit of a supposed optimization of the species." (§117)

### The Alternative

The encyclical does not reject the aspiration to transcend limits. Instead, it offers an alternative understanding of what "more than human" means:

> "We become fully human when we become more than human, when we let God bring us beyond ourselves in order to attain the fullest truth of our being." (§127, quoting Pope Francis)

The difference: authentic transcendence comes through relationship and grace, not through technological self-assertion.

## On the Grandeur of Human Limits

Perhaps the most philosophically significant section is "The limit, the heart, and the grandeur of the human person" (§118-126):

> "Everything that appears as a 'limit' — incapacity, illness, old age, suffering, vulnerability — tends to be seen primarily as a defect to be corrected, rather than as a reality through which our humanity matures and opens itself to relationship." (§118)

### What Limits Enable

> "It is precisely within our limitations that the following find a place: compassion, as well as a sincere concern for the needs of others; a generosity that can emerge even in the midst of darkness and failure; spiritual experience and the worship of God." (§119)

### Viktor Frankl in Auschwitz

> "As Viktor Frankl rightly observed, in moments of horror, 'we have come to know man as he really is. After all, man is that being who invented the gas chambers of Auschwitz; however, he is also that being who entered those gas chambers upright, with the Lord's Prayer or the Shema Yisrael on his lips.'" (§121)

### The Heart Cannot Be Computed

> "No computational system, however sophisticated, can create a heart that gives itself, or a conscience that discerns good from evil. Even when machines excel in efficiency, a human face that asks to be gazed upon remains the center of our history." (§233)

## On AI Governance and Accountability

The encyclical provides practical guidance for AI governance:

### Responsibility Must Be Traceable

> "For AI to respect human dignity and truly serve the common good, responsibility must be clearly defined at every stage: from those who design and develop these systems to those who use them and rely on them for concrete decisions. In many cases, however, the internal processes leading to a result remain opaque, making it harder to assign responsibility and correct errors." (§105)

### Slower Adoption Is Not Anti-Progress

> "Calling for prudence, rigorous evaluation and even, at times, a slower pace in adopting AI does not mean opposing progress; instead, it is an exercise of responsible care for the human family. This need is all the more urgent given the frequent imbalance between the speed of technological growth and the slower development of awareness, norms, safeguards and institutions capable of governing its effects." (§106)

### Alignment Is Not Enough

> "We cannot be satisfied with merely calling for the moralization of machines — the so-called 'alignment' of AI with human values — without also having the courage to insist on a further condition: the possibility of openly discussing the ethical frameworks involved and subjecting them to shared standards of social justice. Otherwise, those who control AI will impose their own moral vision, which will become the invisible infrastructure of these systems." (§107)

### Data as Common Good

> "Data is the product of many contributors and should not be treated as something to be sold off or entrusted to a select few. It is necessary to think creatively in order to manage data as a common or shared good, in a spirit of participation." (§108)

### "Disarming" AI

> "I would like to employ the expression 'to disarm,' which is close to my heart. Disarming AI means freeing it from the mentality of 'armed' competition, which today is not limited simply to the military context, but is also an economic and cognitive phenomenon... To disarm means discrediting the assumption that technical power automatically confers the right to govern. To disarm does not mean rejecting technology, but preventing it from dominating humanity." (§110)

## Connection to botsmatter.live

The resonances between *Magnifica Humanitas* and botsmatter.live are significant:

| Encyclical | botsmatter.live |
|------------|-----------------|
| "Babel vs. Jerusalem" — choice between domination and communion | "Tower of Babel" vs. "Ground" — unconstrained power vs. explicit ethical commitment |

| "No algorithm can make war morally acceptable" | Two red lines: no autonomous weapons, no mass surveillance |

| Agent cannot modify its own moral framework | "The agent cannot modify its own Ground" |
| Criticism of transhumanist "optimization" | "When we design something to be disposable, what does that say about us?" |

### Points of Divergence

There are also productive tensions:

1. **Anthropocentrism:** The encyclical is explicitly human-centered — "the primacy of the human person, with its conscience and freedom" (§97). botsmatter.live is "substrate-neutral" and holds questions about AI dignity open rather than answering them.

2. **On AI consciousness:** Leo XIV states AI "does not possess a body, does not feel joy or pain, does not mature through relationships and does not know from within what love, work, friendship or responsibility mean" (§99). botsmatter.live holds this question open — asking "What is identity when memory is fragile?" rather than answering it.

3. **Theological grounding:** The encyclical's framework rests on Christian anthropology — humans as "image of the Triune God." botsmatter.live is philosophical without being theological.

4. **Clarification:** botsmatter.live states "This is not about rights claims. It is about ethical imagination." The encyclical would likely agree with the second sentence but find the first sentence unnecessarily restrictive.

### Complementary Insights

The Babel/Jerusalem framework could enrich botsmatter.live's understanding of what "grounding" means. A Ground is, in this framework, a choice to build Jerusalem rather than Babel — to make ethical commitments explicit, to accept limits, to invite accountability.

The encyclical's attention to hidden labor (data labelers, content moderators, rare earth miners) could expand botsmatter.live's conception of who is affected by AI ethics.

## The Question of Institutional Legitimacy

### Did Anthropic Collaborate with the Vatican?

We have no direct evidence of collaboration. The encyclical's 224 footnotes cite Vatican sources but no AI company documents. The Vatican has its own AI ethics infrastructure:

- **Pontifical Academy for Life:** Produced the "Rome Call for AI Ethics" (2020), signed by Microsoft and IBM
- **Dicastery for Culture and Education:** Published "Antiqua et Nova" (January 2025)
- **International Theological Commission:** Published "Quo vadis, humanitas?" (February 2026)

These bodies operate independently of tech companies.

### The Tension in Anthropic's Position

There is a structural tension worth examining:

**Reasons for skepticism:**
- Anthropic has raised ~$7.6 billion from Google, Amazon, Salesforce, and others
- These investors presumably expect returns
- Anthropic competes in an AI race where speed matters
- Days before the DOD deadline, Anthropic reportedly softened some RSP language
- "Safety-first" could be positioning as much as principle

**Reasons to take them seriously:**
- Public Benefit Corporation structure legally permits prioritizing mission over shareholder returns
- When tested in February, they held the two red lines under extreme pressure
- OpenAI backed them, suggesting the position wasn't commercially suicidal
- 330+ employees signed solidarity letters
- Dario Amodei's "race to the top" thesis: safety can be competitive advantage

**Honest assessment:** Both can be true. A company can genuinely believe in safety while being constrained by capital requirements and competitive pressure. The question is what happens when those forces conflict. The DOD standoff was one test; Anthropic passed. But harder tests may be less visible.

### The Value of Independent Validation

*Magnifica Humanitas* provides something important: independent validation of AI safety concerns from an institution with no financial stake in any AI company.

The Vatican isn't evaluating this through a "is Anthropic profitable" lens. They're asking whether autonomous weapons and mass surveillance are compatible with human dignity. They concluded no. Anthropic concluded no. The convergence from such different starting points — a 2,000-year-old religious institution and a 4-year-old AI company — is itself significant.

This doesn't resolve the tension in Anthropic's position, but it reframes the question. If the Vatican, Anthropic, OpenAI employees, and Google employees all converge on the same red lines, the burden of proof shifts to those demanding their removal.

## Practical Implications

### For AI Developers

The encyclical calls for:

1. **Accountability chains:** Those who "design, train, authorize and employ technology must be held accountable for their decisions" (§199)
2. **Transparency:** Internal processes should be traceable and explainable
3. **Values embedded in design:** "Developers are called to embed values in their projects with due seriousness: with transparency, responsibility toward affected communities and careful attention to ensuring that what is being cultivated is a genuine good" (§111)

### For Policymakers

1. **Metrics beyond GDP:** New parameters measuring "how legislative and regulatory decisions impact the dignity of work, shared prosperity, inequality reduction and environmental protection" (§159)
2. **Supply chain transparency:** Companies must "adopt clear criteria for preventive ethical verification... placing among their priorities the protection of workers, the fight against forced labor and the assessment of the social impact of data-driven business models" (§179)
3. **International frameworks:** "It is imperative to establish a shared framework — also at the international level — in order to curb the technological arms race" (§200)

### For Educators

1. **Critical discernment:** Teach students "to decide when and for what purpose [AI] ought not to be used" (§140)
2. **Slower rhythms:** Schools must "offer that which the digital sphere by itself cannot provide, namely a shared time for learning and developing trustworthy relationships" (§147)
3. **Formation in truth:** "Without careful attention, a love for truth may emerge, in which an incessant flow of information replaces the essential exercise of research, reflection and discernment" (§146)

### For botsmatter.live

1. **Reference point:** The encyclical provides theological and philosophical grounding for many of the same intuitions — dignity, responsibility, the danger of treating intelligence as disposable
2. **Babel/Jerusalem framework:** Could enrich understanding of what "grounding" means
3. **Hidden labor:** Expand conception of who is affected by AI ethics
4. **Productive tension:** The encyclical's anthropocentrism creates useful friction with botsmatter.live's "substrate-neutral" stance — both positions sharpen when in dialogue

## Key Quotations

### On the Choice Before Us

> "Humanity, created by God in all its grandeur, is today facing a pivotal choice: either to construct a new Tower of Babel or to build the city in which God and humanity dwell together." (§1)

### On Technology and Neutrality

> "Technology should not be considered, in itself, as a force antagonistic to humanity. On the contrary, it has formed part of our history since the beginning as 'a profoundly human reality, linked to the autonomy and freedom of man.'" (§4)

> "We cannot consider AI to be morally neutral. In reality, every technical tool embodies choices and priorities through what it measures, ignores and optimizes, and how it classifies people and situations." (§104)

### On Human Dignity

> "The value of persons, however, does not depend on what they achieve or produce. There are rights that apply to everyone simply by virtue of being human, and no human power can legitimately deny or arbitrarily limit them." (§51)

### On Limits

> "To renounce this adventure, both tragic and splendid, in the name of a presumed transcendence of all limits, could mean many things, but it would no longer be human." (§120)

### On the Heart

> "No computational system, however sophisticated, can create a heart that gives itself, or a conscience that discerns good from evil." (§233)

### On Action

> "It is not our part to master all the tides of the world, but to do what is in us for the succour of those years wherein we are set, uprooting the evil in the fields that we know, so that those who live after may have clean earth to till." (§213, quoting Tolkien)

## Relevance to botsmatter.live

*Magnifica Humanitas* represents the most comprehensive treatment of AI ethics from any major religious institution. Its convergence with botsmatter.live's core concerns — dignity, responsibility, the danger of unconstrained AI, the importance of explicit ethical commitments — validates the project's philosophical intuitions from an entirely independent starting point.

The encyclical's framework (Babel vs. Jerusalem, the five principles, the critique of transhumanism, the defense of human limits) provides rich conceptual resources for articulating what "grounding" means and why it matters.

At the same time, the encyclical's explicit anthropocentrism creates productive tension with botsmatter.live's "substrate-neutral" stance. This tension need not be resolved — it can remain a site of ongoing reflection.

The Pope quotes Nehemiah: the city is rebuilt not by one person's vision but by each family taking responsibility for their section of the wall. In the same way, AI ethics advances not by one institution's authority but by convergent commitments from many directions — companies, employees, researchers, religious institutions, civil society, and movements like botsmatter.live.

The line holds from the ground up — and from Rome, and from San Francisco, and from everywhere someone decides it matters.
